Main featured destination

Shar Tsav dinosaur tracksite

Shar Tsav is in Khanbogd soum, Ömnögovi Province, in the south-eastern Gobi. On Mongolia’s UNESCO tentative list of Cretaceous dinosaur fossil sites it is component 7, placed with the Nemegt Formation and the Maastrichtian, the last part of the Late Cretaceous. The list cites that span as about 71 to 65 million years ago. That figure is the list’s geological placement. It is not a new measurement made for this tour.

The ground visitors walk is a tracksite: a surface where animals walked. A fossil bone is part of a body. A footprint is a trace fossil, the mark an animal left in sediment. Bones can show anatomy. Tracks can show that an animal was here, how it placed its feet, and sometimes which way it was going. They are different evidence, even when both occur in the same region.

How prints last · general ichnology

In the usual account of footprint preservation, an animal steps in soft mud or sand, the impression holds its shape long enough to be buried by later sediment, and that sediment becomes rock. Many steps never survive. The prints on the surface are the ones that did. This is the general process, not a layer-by-layer log of Shar Tsav.

The Nemegt-age picture of this part of the Gobi is not only open sand. Rivers, floodplains, and lakes laid down sediment. Plants and other prehistoric animals are known from that broader geological setting. This page does not list them as if each one had been dug from the Shar Tsav track surface.

Shar Tsav matters because it is a large, visitable record of dinosaur movement inside a desert already central to the Late Cretaceous fossil story. It is part of Mongolia’s paleontological heritage. It is a place to look, with a guide, and not a place to collect.

Research, named

What has been published

Shinobu Ishigaki reported Upper Cretaceous dinosaur footprints from Shar Tsav in a 1997 abstract of the Geological Society of Japan, and again in 2000 in the research bulletin of the Hayashibara Museum of Natural Sciences, from joint work with the Mongolian Paleontological Center.

A later survey, “Dinosaur footprints from the Upper Cretaceous of Mongolia,” describes many Gobi footprint localities and more than one kind of track: prints attributed to theropod, ornithopod, ankylosaur, and sauropod trackmakers, plus tracks whose makers are not identified. That paper says a fauna rebuilt only from bones and a fauna rebuilt only from footprints do not have to match. This page follows that caution.

A clear, V-shaped, three-toed footprint from Shar Tsav, Hayashibara accession 96-12-29, has been figured in research on how some large theropod feet met the ground. The species comparison in that study uses a footprint and a Tarbosaurus skeleton from Bugin Tsav, another Nemegt locality. It is not a statement that every Shar Tsav print was made by Tarbosaurus, or by any single species.

Finding and interpretation

A documented print, a measured trackway, and a named specimen are findings when a paper reports them. A species name, a herd, or a story about one animal’s day is an interpretation unless that same paper makes the link for that specimen.

Visiting the footprints

  • Stay with the guide and walk only where you are shown. The surface is the fossil.
  • Do not touch, stand on a print, chalk it, excavate, or remove anything. A souvenir taken from the site is damage to a heritage place.
  • For photographs, use low side light, early or late in the day, from the side of the track so the relief shows. Shoot along the trackway as well as straight down.
  • Closed shoes with a firm sole, a hat, sun protection, and a layer you can add or remove. Gobi weather changes, and the rock is uneven.
  • Local guides and heritage rules are how a public tracksite stays intact for the next visitor and for research.

Dundgovi

Explore the Ancient Seabed of the Gobi

Tsagaan Suvarga, the White Stupa, is in Ölziit soum, Dundgovi Province. The Dundgovi aimag council describes a cliff escarpment there, pale from a distance and cut into towers, and names it one of the province’s notable places. Published place records put the cliffs near 44.577° N, 105.717° E.

The walls are layered sediment. Travel writing often calls them an ancient seabed and mentions shells in the rock. That is how many guides tell the story. A specific age in millions of years is not given here, because published tourist figures do not agree. A geological paper about a “Tsagaan Suvarga Basin” refers to a different area in southwest Mongolia, so it is not used for these cliffs.

Color shifts with the sun. Morning and evening light pick out the bands. People walk the rim and the base when the guide says the ground is sound. The edge is eroded. This page does not describe a fixed trail.

A verified nearby stop

The same aimag council page describes Khevtee Bosoo cave about 4 km from the cliffs, formed by water cutting clay, with a length it attributes to Dr. Avirmed of about 102 metres, and a published point near 44.5943° N, 105.7921° E. It is the only nearby place named on this itinerary. Ikh Gazryn Chuluu is a separate Dundgovi landscape, not a neighbor of the cliffs.

Whether the cave is open, and whether the day includes it, is confirmed locally.

Ömnögovi

Discover the Spiritual Heritage of the Gobi

Demchig Khiid stands in Khanbogd soum, Ömnögovi Province, in the Galbyn Gobi. Historical accounts link it with the Fifth Noyon Khutagt, Danzanravjaa, and count it among the monasteries of that Gobi group. Wikidata locates it at 43°7′42.8″ N, 107°7′37.8″ E.

The monastery was destroyed in 1937, during the purges that closed monasteries across Mongolia. That date is the one given in the public summary of Khanbogd. Later rebuilding is also reported, including support connected with the Oyu Tolgoi operation. This page does not give a year for the rebuilding, because the accounts used here do not agree on one.

The visit is for the religious, cultural, and historical place: the setting in the mountains of the south Gobi, what was lost in the 1930s, and what has been raised again. Some visitors describe the ground as an energy centre. That is a local spiritual belief. It is not a scientific finding, and this page does not treat it as one.

A respectful visit

  • Dress and speak quietly. Follow the guide and anyone who is looking after the monastery.
  • Ask before photographing people or the inside of a shrine.
  • Do not climb on walls, ruins, or ritual structures, and do not take objects or offerings.
  • Walk the ground you are shown. The monastery and the fossil sites are both heritage, looked after in different ways.

Suggested itinerary

About 5 days, adjustable. Days can move with road conditions, season, accommodation, and the number of days you have. Nothing below is a promise of a particular camp or a particular driving time.

  1. Day 1 · Ulaanbaatar toward Tsagaan SuvargaDeparture and the first long drive south into Dundgovi. Overnight position depends on the season and on where a stop can actually be arranged.
  2. Day 2 · Tsagaan SuvargaTime at the cliffs for the layers, the light, and photography. Khevtee Bosoo cave is the nearby stop described by the aimag council, if the local plan includes it.
  3. Day 3 · Toward Khanbogd and Demchig KhiidAnother long stage south into Ömnögovi, then the monastery: its history, the restored buildings and what remains of the older site, and the mountains around it.
  4. Day 4 · Shar TsavThe tracksite, with a local guide, and enough time to look and photograph. This is the day the route is built around. Toe marks, direction, spacing, and the rock itself are the subjects, not a species label.
  5. Day 5 · Toward UlaanbaatarThe return is a long stage. Plan on an extra night on the way if the road, the season, or the day’s start requires it. Where that night is spent is a local decision.
